# Naming screen: 40 best names

Screened from 1,505 machine candidates plus 26 hand-made names. Linguistic screening only: no trademark, domain or
App Store search was done, so every "risk" below is a linguist's flag, not a clearance.

How the pool was cut down:
- Whole roots removed: `lien` (EN: a legal claim on property), `louch` (EN "louche": shady), `mail` (Maille
  mustard, e-mail), `pak` (a slur sits one letter away in EN), `tut`/`tout` (tutu, toutou), `tour` (Tourette),
  `nex`/`link`/`omni`/`cycl`/`enti` (cold corporate sounds), `suiv`/`sui`/`cui` (unreadable for an American).
- Words that already exist or belong to a brand removed: Toto, Fino, Omnium, Ricola, Domino, Panko, Panino, Plato,
  Miami, Rolo-like forms, Milo, Hopi, Loon, Looka, Orbit-like forms, Zesta, Tina/Tino/Remi and other first names.
- Descriptive names (meal, ring, rest-over) are kept only at the bottom of the list, as a reference point.
- Respelling: Bouklo is Bouclo with a k, so the attack is crisp and an American reads it right away. No other name
  needed respelling: every machine name below appears in `candidates.json` exactly as written.

## Three patterns that worked

**1. Words that already mean "and next!" in everyday speech** (Hopla, Bisette, Lasuite, Encorée, Rondo, Coda). They
borrow a gesture or a musical figure rather than describing the product, so the brief's line "an idea, not a
description" holds, and the story ("the cream hops to Wednesday", "the theme comes back") takes one sentence to
tell. The music and interjection roots ended up stronger than the literal chain roots (link, nex, seq), which all
sounded corporate.

**2. One soft root plus one crisp syllable, 5 or 6 letters** (Loopot, Bouklo, Fondo, Platou, Finou, Brika). This
pattern hits the target sound exactly: a round ou/o/a vowel with m/l/n, plus a single p, k, t or b that gives the
"smart, decisive" edge. It is also why most machine endings fell away: -ix, -um and -ette made names that were too
technical or too cute. The plain endings -o, -a and -ou carried them.

**3. A cooking object turned into a verb** (Loopot, Tandish, Platour, Potée, Mijota). Using kitchen nouns (pot,
plat, dish) reassures the careful mother that this is real home cooking. Turning them into motion (loop, tandem,
tour) gives the 21-year-old the cleverness. The best names do both in one word, and Loopot is the closest heir to
the Mealoop feeling the client liked.
